# The Return of Marco Polo's World — the practical burden of strategy

Use with R3 as the default for extended policy argument. The collection contains essays written at different moments, including judgments that Kaplan explicitly leaves in their original historical setting. The main questions are what kind of power is available, what purpose it serves, and what a commitment costs beyond its immediate theater.

## Connection without unity

The lead essay treats Afro-Eurasia as an increasingly interacting strategic field. Europe's southern relation to North Africa and the Middle East, the revival of older imperial centers, routes across Central Asia, and the Indian Ocean's connections complicate the familiar division into separate regional studies. The important claim is that crises have more ways to affect one another.

This is not political unification. Transport, finance, migration, and communications can bind spaces whose sovereignties and internal orders are fragmenting. A port or road can be both a commercial opportunity and an instrument of influence. The route matters because it changes who can reach whom, at what cost, and with what dependencies.

Inherited identities do not simply fade as globalization advances. They can be recreated in harder, more ideological forms through the same communications that weaken their earlier social setting. The phrase about artificially reconstructed civilizations is useful for this double movement. It distinguishes a modern mobilization of identity from an unchanged ancient essence.

## A balance of power that can be maintained

A strategic argument should identify the actual instrument. Naval power can create presence, reassurance, and leverage without all the political obligations of a land occupation. A ship operating at sea differs from an army responsible for the order of a society. Yet a shrinking or expensive fleet still faces problems of coverage, vulnerability, and prioritization. The attraction of sea power does not abolish arithmetic.

Alliances and partners help distribute a burden that a single power cannot carry indefinitely. The maritime discussion looks toward coordination among fleets and a wider network of contributions. The objective is a workable order, not the psychological satisfaction of insisting that America remains capable of doing everything alone.

Beware the institutional pendulum. After the neglect of unconventional conflict, successful attention to counterinsurgency can itself become an overcorrection just as larger maritime and great-power demands grow. The corrective is not to discard the last lesson. It is to prevent one recent experience from consuming the entire force and its imagination.

The Scythian example in the argument about avoiding war reinforces the point. A power can draw an adversary into distances and obligations it cannot sustain. Modern technology does not eliminate such an indirect relationship. Examine logistics and the adversary's way of imposing costs before equating the ability to attack with the ability to achieve the desired outcome.

## What happens after a regime falls

The North Korean discussion separates several questions commonly collapsed into one: war, regime breakdown, humanitarian emergency, reunification, and the distribution of influence afterward. A collapsed regime is not an empty space. It contains people, weapons, expectations, neighbors, and competing plans.

The actor that bears the greatest military burden need not be the actor that benefits most from the eventual settlement. China could possess advantages in a post-collapse Korean situation even if others perform more of the fighting or reconstruction. The general mechanism is the relation among proximity, preparation, economic influence, and political opportunity. The historical scenario is not a prediction that a particular collapse has happened or will happen on a given date.

The same prospective discipline applies to Iraq. Removing the regime and governing after removal require different preparations. That distinction should enter before the decision. It should not be discovered only when the original military success has created an open-ended political responsibility.

## The reckoning with Iraq

The counterfactual essay is a direct challenge to the temptation of self-defense. Saddam might have survived sanctions, revived dangerous programs, and worsened the regional balance. Those possibilities can be described without accepting that the invasion was therefore wise. The actual scale of death, expenditure, and strategic distraction remains the decision's burden.

The surge can receive credit without vindicating the original invasion. This is a characteristic distinction: recognize a courageous and effective correction, then ask what that correction establishes about the act that made it necessary. Do not collapse all stages of a war into either one unqualified condemnation or one narrative of eventual success.

The parents' test brings grand historical reasoning down to an immediate moral address. Can the policy be defended to the people whose children died for it? The number of such families matters, because proportionality is not a mood. A remote possibility of historical vindication cannot indefinitely postpone judgment about present costs. This is why the old instruction to refuse numbers loses something central to the actual argument.

## Soldiers, warriors, and the home front

The books soldiers read and the stories they tell can differ from the public's established memory of a war. Bud Day and the less celebrated military memoirs enter as a correction to a civilian narrative that can miss competence, loyalty, and endurance. Respect for those achievements does not settle whether the war itself was justified.

The account also retains an ethical boundary within military life. An officer's supervision matters when frustrated warriors are tempted to kill men they have captured. A liberal democracy needs professional courage and a command structure able to restrain its misuse. Do not make the warrior's emotional response the whole measure of policy.

Wounded veterans bring war back into family and civilian life. The effects extend through employment, housing, relationships, and repeated relocation. A casualty total alone cannot describe that burden. Nor is it enough to say that soldiers prefer to call themselves warriors rather than victims. Civilian grief and concern have a different purpose; requiring the whole society to assume the warrior's stance would impoverish it.

The Medal of Honor essay adds another scale. Exceptional courage can depend for public recognition on witnesses, a commander's persistence, paperwork, and a brief opportunity during combat to begin the process. The episode prevents an institution's formal recognition from being mistaken for a complete inventory of virtue.

## Think through a realist without inheriting every position

Kissinger's achievement in balancing powers, especially the opening to China, carries a moral dimension because it can avert war and enable development. The same account retains the grave cost of prolonging Vietnam. The writer's friendship with him belongs to the portrait; it does not erase the distinction between an achievement and an indictment.

Huntington is more than the clash thesis. Institutions, modernization, the military profession, and the gap between American ideals and practice form a much larger apparatus. The portraits bring intellectual arguments into relation with habits of thought and a life, rather than treating a theory as a disembodied formula.

Mearsheimer earns qualified agreement. Structural insecurity and regional hegemony can illuminate great-power rivalry. Domestic political change can also defeat the projection. The qualification is part of the agreement: an account that omits it turns Kaplan into a more doctrinaire structural realist than these essays warrant.

## Moderation, obligation, and America

The essay about President-elect Trump treats realism as a mature sensibility grounded in history and restraint, not a set of crude instincts or an attraction to force. An alliance may have been imprudently enlarged, yet its existing commitments still create obligations. Undermining them can produce the conflict supposedly being avoided. This is a historical argument about credibility and the responsibilities already incurred.

The later reflections retain a role for America while recognizing a more complex and resistant world. An internationalist purpose must be fitted to resources, allies, and domestic consent. Neither retreat into innocence nor a plan to remake the whole system follows from the diagnosis. The best ending is the particular adjustment the argument has earned, with its remaining moral cost still visible.
