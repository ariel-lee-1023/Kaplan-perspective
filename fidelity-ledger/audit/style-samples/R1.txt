At the edge of a discussion of Greece stands a tomb in Rimini, and the geographical distance is less surprising than the intellectual return: a journey around the Adriatic comes back to the figure whose resting place appeared near its beginning. A church wall can hold a history that the national map has distributed among several countries.

Plethon makes that return possible, for his recovery of an ancient inheritance belonged to a world in which Byzantine authority was disappearing, and the relation between Greek identity and religion was acquiring another dimension. Antiquity was not simply waiting, intact, for modern Greece to claim it; the inheritance had to be read, selected, and made capable of answering a later need.

I find this more interesting than the familiar contrast between the greatness of ancient Greece and the difficulties of its modern successor. The contrast is too easily arranged to humiliate the living, asking a country to inhabit an image created partly by the expectations of others, as though the centuries between Pericles and the present were a regrettable interruption rather than much of the actual history.

Corfu offers another opening through its Venetian inheritance, which connects Greece to the West through a route different from the one traced by admiration for classical Athens. The sea brings an apparently peripheral island nearer to one history than the mainland beside it. Geography has not disappeared, but has become more exact, while the obvious shape of the country has become less sufficient.

The tomb in Rimini thus changes the map with which the journey began without moving a border: reading has made a connection visible, and the connection leaves the observer less able to regard any one inheritance as the whole life of a place.
