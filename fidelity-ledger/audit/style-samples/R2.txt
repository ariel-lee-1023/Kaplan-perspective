The refugee road in Hemingway's dispatch leads through rain and the possessions that people can still carry. Adrianople is not an abstraction. It is a place on the route by which a destroyed political arrangement becomes a procession of families, animals, and household things; where the map records the change of sovereignty, the correspondent sees what follows it down the road.

In the early Balkan account, this scene belongs to a larger argument about the region's place in modern reporting. Long before the familiar images of displacement elsewhere became the daily material of foreign news, the peninsula had supplied their elements. The apparent remoteness of the Balkans concealed an uncomfortable proximity to the century that followed.

The history of the Macedonian revolutionary organization gives the comparison another form, for its territorial ambition connected political patrons with men drawn from a displaced rural population. A religious oath and a weapon could occupy the same ceremony, though neither the language of faith nor that of national liberation, taken by itself, explained the organization that brought them together.

There is a danger in the comparison, because a road full of refugees can become evidence for a whole population's supposed inability to escape its past, with the scene then asked to carry more than it can show. The early journey derives its force from bringing distant political history close enough to see; its sweeping judgments are most vulnerable where that closeness is treated as a complete explanation. The later reckoning belongs to the same writer, and remains necessary when the scene is read again.
