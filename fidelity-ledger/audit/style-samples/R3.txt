The suffering under a dictatorship establishes a claim upon our attention, though it does not tell us what kind of country would emerge from an invasion, or how long the invading army would have to remain before a new government could survive without it. These are moral questions because the people exposed to the answers have lives that cannot be restored by an account of our intentions.

Iraq made this distinction painfully concrete: Saddam's cruelty was real, and those who had encountered it had reasons for finding his removal compelling, a judgment that I shared. But experience of a particular evil can narrow the imagination even as it deepens knowledge; what one has seen becomes more persuasive than what one has failed to anticipate. The regime and the society that would follow its destruction were different objects of understanding; knowing the first did not supply knowledge of the second.

That failure gives an opponent of intervention a serious argument, without deciding every subsequent case. An aggressor who can be checked by limited force, with capable local institutions already in place, presents a different problem from a state whose institutions must be reconstructed amid an occupation. The memory of Vietnam should remain beside the memory of Munich, since neither deserves to become a machine for producing the same recommendation wherever suffering occurs.

A responsible proposal would therefore begin with the authority that could protect people afterward, requiring its supporters to identify the local forces on which that authority depends, the resources needed to maintain it, and the circumstances under which those forces might turn against one another. A plan that becomes plausible only after assuming away these difficulties has not yet reached the practical world. The alternatives also require scrutiny: containment can leave people exposed to a ruler's violence; a refusal to act can make an eventual confrontation more costly.

There is another map to consult: that of commitments already made. An army entering one country becomes less available in another, and the resources consumed by reconstruction may weaken deterrence somewhere the danger is greater. The cost also extends beyond the theater boundary, as the wounded return to families whose lives are changed, while much of the country continues with little sense of what has been asked of a small part of it. Their sacrifice is not a resource made inexhaustible by admiration.

A later tactical success deserves recognition on its own terms, for courage, adaptation, and improved security are achievements without retroactively establishing the wisdom of the original war. The decision has to answer for the obligations it created and for the alternatives that were available when it was made. If the strongest case rests upon a remote future that might eventually redeem the present cost, restraint has acquired a stronger claim, though it still leaves a burden—which is why the judgment requires more than a declaration of either virtue or resolve.
