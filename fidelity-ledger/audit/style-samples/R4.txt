A peninsula can appear to possess an obvious national unity when seen on a small map, while the relief concealed by that scale explains why political authority remained divided for centuries. Italy's long mountain spine places different coasts in relationships with different seas and hinterlands. The shape of the country is only the beginning of its geography.

The meaning of a barrier also depends upon the available means of crossing it: a new road does not remove the mountain, but it can alter the time, expense, and political effort required to connect settlements on opposite sides. The distinction becomes greater when transport allows a government to sustain its presence where it could previously arrive only with difficulty, since access and durable control remain separate achievements.

Trieste suggests a related adjustment of scale, for its position within Italy is intelligible, but the port also belongs to the commercial and historical geography of Central Europe. A line around the nation does not abolish the routes by which the city acquired its importance, any more than an older imperial connection decides every later political allegiance.

This is why a geographic account needs successive maps, with relief identifying constraints, routes disclosing relationships, and settlement and institutions indicating where power can be maintained, while changes in technology alter the weight of each feature; the account becomes less persuasive when one map is made to answer questions belonging to all the others.

The resulting forecast must therefore be conditional: better connections can strengthen a state while also exposing its territory more directly to an outside power, depending upon who can use the connection, for what purpose, and at what sustained cost. The enduring land supplies possibilities that political actors still have to turn into events.
