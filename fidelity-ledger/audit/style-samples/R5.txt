Antigone's brother does not have to be an admirable citizen for her obligation to him to remain real. The claim of the family reaches beyond a public judgment of merit, yet the state, too, protects something that cannot be dismissed merely because its command collides with that obligation. The difficulty begins when both claims have substance.

A decision can preserve one good while destroying another. Success does not make the lost good worthless, and this is the residue that a public account of necessity often tries to remove, because it makes the achievement harder to celebrate and the person responsible less able to rest inside it.

The knowledge should make the next act more careful without making action impossible. Hamlet and Macbeth disclose different dangers in the interval before a decision: thought can become delay, while ambition can make speed feel like destiny. Neither play supplies a rule that leaders should always hesitate or always seize the moment; each brings attention to what is happening within the person who must choose.

Iraq adds a modern burden to this literary understanding, since a vivid knowledge of tyranny did not produce an adequate imagination of anarchy. The error cannot be surrendered to the failures of execution alone. Nor can a distant possibility of vindication release the decision from what it actually cost.

Tragedy nevertheless permits consolation, for a shared form can make suffering less solitary, even when it cannot restore what has been lost. Recognition may return a person to life with greater strength. The point of looking steadily at catastrophe is partly to recover the capacity to prevent another one, and to accept that this work will be done without innocence.
